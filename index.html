About Me

From PhD research to an independent atmospheric-dynamics research programme.

I completed my PhD in Geophysics at Victoria University of Wellington, New Zealand, working with Prof. James Renwick. My thesis, Tipping the scales: The butterfly effect in Atmospheric Blocking and Temperature Extremes, examined how blocking, internal variability, and circulation uncertainty shape extreme-temperature risk.

My research combines large climate-model ensembles, reanalysis, circulation diagnostics, statistical analysis, and high-performance computing. A central theme is separating robust dynamical signals from outcomes that depend strongly on detection choices or internal variability.

I am especially interested in research that connects atmospheric dynamics to predictability and decision-relevant climate risk: not only whether extremes change, but why particular circulation pathways make some outcomes much more likely than others.

Research Projects & Scientific Contributions

The questions I have led, the tools I built or combined, and what each project contributes.

Published · Southern Hemisphere dynamics

How much does the definition of “blocking” change what we think the climate system is doing?

Question. Southern Hemisphere blocking is notoriously sensitive to detection method. I tested how reversal- and anomaly-based definitions alter the diagnosed climatology and model evaluation.

Approach. CESM2-LENS2 (100 members), reanalysis, spatial blocking diagnostics, persistence and area constraints, and method-to-method comparison.

Contribution. The work separates method-sensitive conclusions from more robust projected signals, providing a clearer basis for interpreting blocking biases and future change.

Output: Geophysical Research Letters (2026)

Manuscript in preparation for resubmission · Northern Hemisphere

Can large-ensemble blocking biases be traced back to the circulation itself?

Question. Rather than only documenting where a model under- or over-produces blocking, this project asks what large-scale circulation structures help explain that spatial bias.

Approach. A hybrid Northern Hemisphere blocking index applied across CESM2-LENS2, with circulation and jet-stream diagnostics used to interpret regional model behaviour.

Contribution. The aim is to connect blocking-frequency errors to physically interpretable dynamical structure, moving from model evaluation toward process attribution.

Status: Northern Hemisphere blocking assessment being prepared for resubmission.

Current research · Climate extremes

Why can blocked heat risk remain high even when blocking itself becomes less frequent?

Question. Changes in event frequency do not necessarily translate directly into changes in risk. I examine how warming alters the severity and conditional probability of heat and cold extremes associated with blocking.

Approach. Compound blocked heat/cold events, large-ensemble risk ratios, lead–lag analysis, intensity metrics, and decomposition of change into frequency and conditional-risk effects.

Contribution. This framework distinguishes how often the circulation state occurs from how damaging the associated temperature outcome becomes in a warmer climate.

Status: Manuscript development from PhD Chapters 3–4.

PhD contribution · Uncertainty & confidence

When is an apparent change in blocking or extreme risk actually robust?

Question. Large ensembles can produce striking spatial patterns simply through internal variability. This project asks where projected changes are strong enough to distinguish from that background spread.

Approach. CESM2-LENS2 internal-variability sampling, ensemble-based confidence assessment, spatial significance testing, and false-discovery-rate control.

Contribution. The framework produces spatial confidence information for blocking and blocked extremes, helping separate robust climate signals from locations where ensemble uncertainty remains dominant.

Value: A reusable uncertainty framework for circulation diagnostics and compound climate-extreme analysis.

Next research direction · Predictability & rare events

Where are the dynamical leverage points that make two nearly identical atmospheres diverge?

Research direction. I want to use targeted branching ensembles around high-impact events to identify when small circulation differences begin to amplify into very different extreme outcomes.

Proposed framework. Branch simulations before an event, diagnose divergence in blocking, jets and Rossby-wave structure, then re-branch around candidate dynamical leverage points.

Why it matters. This links the “butterfly effect” idea in my PhD to a practical predictability framework for rare extremes and offers a bridge between dynamical meteorology, large ensembles and emerging data-driven methods.
